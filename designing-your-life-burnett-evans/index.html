Core Framework & Mental Models

The vocabulary Burnett and Evans build early in the book, and the central tension the whole approach argues from.

Design Thinking

A problem-solving approach borrowed from product design: get curious about the problem, generate many possible solutions before judging any of them, and build something cheap and testable rather than reasoning your way to a conclusion in your head.

Wayfinding

Navigating toward a life without a fixed destination, using markers of engagement and energy — not a rigid five-year plan — to tell you whether you're headed somewhere good.

Dysfunctional Belief

A widely held idea about work or life that sounds true but actually blocks you from moving forward — e.g., "I should already know what I want to do." Named so it can be replaced with a reframe.

Gravity Problem

A circumstance you can't change no matter how hard you work at it (your age, the economy, a past decision). Gravity problems aren't solved — they're accepted and designed around.

The Central Tension: Life Planning vs. Life Design

The Life-Planning View

  • Set a specific long-term goal and work backward from it.
  • Success means hitting the goal you set years ago.
  • One "right" career or path exists — your job is to find it.
  • Changing direction late means you failed to plan well.
VS

Burnett & Evans's Life-Design View

  • Follow markers of engagement and energy — wayfinding, not a fixed goal.
  • Success means staying engaged and coherent, even as the path changes.
  • Many workable lives exist for you — design and test a few of them.
  • Changing direction is data, not failure — it's how the process works.

Five Guiding Themes to Track While You Read

  • 1Engagement is the compass. Notice what absorbs you and what drains you — that data (the Good Time Journal) is more reliable than abstract goal-setting.
  • 2Problems must be reframed before they can be solved. A dysfunctional belief disguised as a problem statement will never yield a good answer.
  • 3Generate before you evaluate. Design thinking insists on multiple options (the Odyssey Plans) before choosing — judging too early kills good ideas.
  • 4Prototype cheaply before committing. A short conversation or small experience tells you more than months of deliberation.
  • 5Coherence beats "the best choice." A workable life is one where your Workview, Lifeview, and daily actions align — not the theoretically optimal path.

Close Reading & Technical Deep Dive

A slower, side-by-side pass on the mechanics that make the rest of the book work.

1. Dysfunctional Beliefs vs. Reframes

In Plain Terms
Belief: "I need to figure out my one true passion."
Reframe: Most people don't have a single pre-existing passion; engagement is built through trying things, not discovered by introspection.
Belief: "It's too late to change direction."
Reframe: There's always a version of a good life still available from here; the question is which one, not whether one exists.
Belief: "A career should be a straight line up."
Reframe: Most well-lived careers zigzag; coherence, not linearity, is what makes a path feel right.
Technical Breakdown

A belief functions as dysfunctional when it (a) forecloses exploration before any data is gathered, and (b) can't actually be tested or falsified — it's accepted as background truth. The reframe technique doesn't argue the belief is false outright; it substitutes a more workable belief that reopens the option space, then lets prototyping supply the evidence.

This mirrors a designer's move: don't argue with a client's stated problem — reframe the design brief until it's solvable, then test the reframed version against reality.

2. Gravity Problems vs. Actionable Problems

In Plain Terms

A gravity problem is a circumstance no amount of effort will change — you can't out-work gravity. Examples: you can't be 20 years old again, you can't force a specific person to hire you, you can't undo a market downturn.

An actionable problem, by contrast, responds to effort and iteration: how you present your experience, which industries you target, what skills you build next.

Technical Breakdown

Misclassifying a gravity problem as actionable produces a specific failure mode — repeated effort with no traction, because the problem was never solvable, only acceptable. Misclassifying an actionable problem as gravity produces premature resignation.

The diagnostic test Burnett and Evans propose: ask whether the problem responds at all to a change in approach, not just a change in effort. If varying the approach changes nothing, it's likely gravity — accept it and design around it instead.

3. Scoring the Odyssey Plan — Resonance, Confidence, Resources, Coherence

In Plain Terms

Each of the three Odyssey Plans gets rated on four dimensions: Resonance (does this plan feel like "you" when you imagine living it?), Confidence (how sure are you this is achievable?), Resources (do you have, or can you get, what this requires — money, time, skills, relationships?), and Coherence (does this plan match your Workview and Lifeview?).

Technical Breakdown

The scoring isn't meant to produce a single winning number — a plan can score low on Confidence and still be worth pursuing, since confidence typically rises after prototyping, not before it. The real value of the exercise is comparative: it surfaces which trade-offs you're actually making between plans, rather than letting one plan win by being the only one you considered.

Reference & Glossary

Core terms and the book's named tools and exercises.

Core Vocabulary
Design Thinking
A method favoring curiosity, generating many options, and testable prototypes over pure analysis — borrowed from product design and applied to life decisions.
Wayfinding
Navigating toward a good life using markers of engagement and energy rather than a fixed long-term goal.
Engagement
The degree to which an activity absorbs your attention — a primary signal for what to do more of.
Energy
Whether an activity leaves you fueled or drained, independent of how enjoyable it seemed in the moment.
Dysfunctional Belief
A widely held idea about work or life that sounds true but blocks exploration before any evidence is gathered.
Reframe
A substitute belief that reopens the option space a dysfunctional belief had closed off.
Gravity Problem
A circumstance that doesn't respond to effort or a change in approach — accepted and designed around, not solved.
Actionable Problem
A problem that does respond to effort, a change in approach, or iteration — a legitimate target for design.
Coherence
The degree to which your Workview, Lifeview, and daily choices actually align with each other.
Radical Collaboration
Treating life design as a team sport — actively seeking other people's perspectives and experience rather than deciding in isolation.
Bias to Action
Preferring a small real-world test over further deliberation whenever a decision is stuck in analysis.
Failure Immunity
Distinguishing types of "failure" (a screwup, a weakness, or ordinary growth) so setbacks inform the next iteration instead of stopping it.
Tools From the Book
Good Time Journal
A daily log rating activities on engagement and energy, kept for several weeks to surface real patterns instead of guesses.
Workview
A short written statement of what work is for — money, growth, contribution, status — checked against your Lifeview.
Lifeview
A short written statement of what life is for — meaning, relationships, purpose — checked against your Workview.
Odyssey Plan
Three distinct five-year plans (current path, alternate path, wildcard path) sketched side-by-side before choosing among or combining them.
Resonance
An Odyssey Plan rating for how much a plan feels like an authentic "you" when you imagine living it.
Confidence (rating)
An Odyssey Plan rating for how achievable a plan currently seems — expected to rise after prototyping, not before.
Resources (rating)
An Odyssey Plan rating for whether you have, or can realistically get, what the plan requires.
Prototype Conversation
A structured conversation (a "Life Design Interview") with someone already living a version of a path you're considering.
Prototype Experience
A short, low-stakes trial of a path — a weekend, a volunteer stint, a side project — run before committing years to it.
Anchor Problem
The real underlying problem once a dysfunctional belief has been reframed — the thing actually worth designing a solution for.
"Good Enough" Decision-Making
Choosing among well-prototyped options once you have sufficient information, rather than searching indefinitely for a provably optimal choice.
