Core Framework & Mental Models

The vocabulary Mollick builds in the book's first part, and the central tension his advice argues from.

Co-Intelligence

Mollick's term for working with AI as a collaborator rather than treating it as either a replacement or a mere tool. The goal is a partnership in which human judgment and machine capability each cover the other's weak spots.

Alien Mind

Large language models produce human-sounding language, but they are built and trained very differently from a person. Mollick calls them an "alien" kind of intelligence: useful to treat like a person in conversation, but unreliable to assume they share human common sense or motives.

Jagged Frontier

AI's abilities don't form a smooth boundary. It can excel at tasks that seem hard (drafting, summarizing, ideation) and fail at tasks that seem easy, and it's often hard to tell in advance which is which. Knowing where the frontier lies for your work requires testing.

Human in the Loop

Keeping a person responsible for reviewing, correcting, and owning AI output. It guards against confident errors and keeps human skill and accountability in the process.

The Central Tension: AI as Tool vs. AI as Collaborator

The "Software Tool" View

  • AI behaves like any program: predictable, repeatable, with a manual that tells you what it does.
  • Skill means learning the correct commands and settings.
  • Errors are bugs that will be patched, so wait until the product is finished.
  • Adoption is a purchasing and training decision made from the top down.
VS

Mollick's Co-Intelligence View

  • AI is general-purpose and unpredictable at the edges, so no manual covers every case.
  • Skill comes from experimenting and building a feel for where it helps and fails.
  • Errors such as hallucination are part of how these systems work, so human review stays essential.
  • The people closest to the work are best placed to discover valuable uses.

Five Guiding Themes to Track While You Read

  • 1Experience beats theory. You learn what AI can do for your work by using it on real tasks, not by reading about it.
  • 2The frontier is jagged and moves. Strengths and weaknesses don't line up with human intuition about task difficulty, and they shift as models improve.
  • 3Fluent isn't the same as correct. A model can sound authoritative while being wrong, so verification is part of the workflow.
  • 4Delegate by task, not by job. Jobs are bundles of tasks, and AI changes which tasks are worth doing yourself.
  • 5Keep your own skill alive. Over-reliance can dull the very expertise you need to judge AI's output.

Close Reading & Technical Deep Dive

A slower, side-by-side pass on the mechanics that make the rest of the advice work.

1. The Jagged Frontier and "Falling Asleep at the Wheel"

In Plain Terms

In a field study Mollick helped run with consultants at Boston Consulting Group, people using AI did notably better on tasks that fell inside AI's current abilities. On a task deliberately chosen to sit outside them, the AI-assisted group did worse than people working without AI, because they trusted a plausible but wrong answer.

Technical Breakdown

The lesson is not "AI helps" or "AI hurts" but that the sign of the effect depends on which side of the frontier a task sits. Because the frontier is invisible and uneven, the usual signal people rely on, how hard a task feels to a human, is a poor guide. The failure mode Mollick describes is complacency: when output is fluent and usually good, people stop checking it, and errors slip through exactly where the model is weakest.

The practical response is to map the frontier through experiments on your own tasks and to build checks into any workflow that touches the edge.

2. Sorting Work: Just Me, Delegated, and Automated Tasks — Centaurs and Cyborgs

In Plain Terms

Mollick suggests sorting your tasks into three piles. Just Me tasks are ones you keep because they matter to your identity, your learning, or your ethics. Delegated tasks are ones you hand to AI and then review. Automated tasks are ones AI can run with little supervision.

He also describes two working styles: centaurs draw a clear line between what the human does and what the AI does, while cyborgs weave the two together within a single task.

Technical Breakdown

The sorting is a risk-and-value decision made per task. A task moves toward automation as errors become cheap to catch and cost little when missed, and toward "Just Me" as the work builds skill you need or carries consequences you can't outsource. Mollick pairs this with the "best available human" standard: compare AI not to the world's top expert but to the person you could realistically get for the job.

Centaur and cyborg styles are not ranked. Centaur suits tasks with clean handoffs, such as having AI draft and you finalize; cyborg suits iterative work, such as passing a paragraph back and forth until it reads right.

3. Why Language Models Sound Right and Still Get Things Wrong

In Plain Terms

A large language model learns patterns from huge amounts of text and then predicts what words should come next. It is then refined with human feedback so that its answers feel helpful and safe. That recipe produces fluent, confident text, but fluency was never the same thing as checking facts.

Technical Breakdown

Two stages matter here. Pretraining builds broad pattern knowledge, including whatever biases and errors exist in the source text. Reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) shapes behavior toward answers people rate as good, which can reward sounding helpful as much as being right. Hallucination, meaning a plausible but invented claim, follows from a system that generates likely text rather than retrieving verified facts.

Mollick ties this to the alignment problem: getting a system's behavior to match human goals is hard even when it appears to be working. It is also why he says to treat the AI like a person in how you talk to it, while remembering it is not one in how it fails.

Reference & Glossary

Core terms, plus the working styles and task categories Mollick uses.

Core Vocabulary
Co-Intelligence
Working with AI as a collaborator so that human judgment and machine capability cover each other's weaknesses.
Alien Mind
Mollick's description of AI as an intelligence that sounds human but is built and fails very differently from a person.
Large Language Model (LLM)
An AI system trained on large amounts of text to predict and generate language, the technology behind modern chatbots.
Pretraining
The first training stage, in which a model learns broad patterns from vast text, including the biases and errors in that text.
RLHF
Reinforcement learning from human feedback: a refinement stage that steers a model toward answers people rate as helpful and safe.
Hallucination
A fluent, confident output that is false or invented, arising because the model generates likely text rather than verified facts.
Alignment
The challenge of getting an AI system's behavior to match human goals and values, even in situations its designers didn't anticipate.
Jagged Frontier
The uneven boundary of AI's abilities: strong at some hard-seeming tasks, weak at some easy-seeming ones, and hard to predict in advance.
Working With AI
Human in the Loop
Keeping a person responsible for reviewing, correcting, and owning AI output.
Centaur
A work style with a clear division of labor: the human and the AI each take the tasks they're best at.
Cyborg
A work style that intertwines human and AI effort within a single task, passing work back and forth.
Best Available Human
A comparison standard: judge AI against the person you could realistically get for the task, not the world's top expert.
Just Me Tasks
Tasks you keep for yourself because they build your skill, express your identity, or carry ethical weight.
Delegated Tasks
Tasks you hand to AI and then review, keeping responsibility for the result.
Automated Tasks
Tasks AI can run end to end with little supervision because errors are cheap to catch and cost little if missed.
Falling Asleep at the Wheel
Mollick's image for complacency: trusting good-enough AI output so much that you stop checking it, and miss errors where it is weakest.
